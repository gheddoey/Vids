# Muhammad Ali — brief, research, script (screenwriting directive)

## 1. Concept
~60 s vertical biography short (1080×1920, 30 fps), blocky R6 style, six rules. Audience: general, 13+.
Intended emotion: amused by a kid's grudge → thrilled by the rise → angry at what he lost → triumphant
→ quiet, human ending.

## 2. Premise
**The man who would fight anyone, and the one fight he refused.** His whole life starts with a
12-year-old wanting to fight a thief. It turns on the one fight he wouldn't take (the Vietnam draft) and
what it cost him. The irony is the engine: a fighter famous for saying no to a fight.

## 3. Hook & ending
- **Hook:** a stolen bike and a furious 12-year-old threatening the thief. Small, specific, funny, and it
  plants the question: who is this kid?
- **Ending:** after the triumph and the shaking hands, return to the bike: "He never found the kid who
  stole his bike." A callback that shrinks the legend back to the boy, and leaves the grudge that started
  everything forever unresolved.
- Setups → payoffs: the bike (line 1 → line 9); "learn to fight first" (line 2) → "the one fight he
  refused" (line 5); "twenty-two" title (line 3) → "Twenty-two years later" (line 8).

## 5. Script (174 words ≈ 60 s at ~2.9 words/s) — `tools/scripts/ali.json`
1. Louisville, 1954. Someone steals a twelve-year-old's bike, and he tells a cop he's going to whup the thief.
2. The cop runs a boxing gym. Learn to fight first, he says.
3. Six years later, Cassius Clay is an Olympic champion. At twenty-two, he takes the heavyweight title from the most feared man in boxing.
4. Then he changes his name to Muhammad Ali. And when the Army drafts him in 1967, he refuses to step forward.
5. They strip his title, take his license, and sentence him to five years. The man who'd fight anyone loses three and a half years of his prime over the one fight he refused.
6. He comes back slower, and Joe Frazier hands him his first loss. Months later, the Supreme Court clears him, eight to nothing.
7. Zaire, 1974. He leans on the ropes, lets George Foreman punch himself out, and knocks him down in the eighth. Champion again.
8. Twenty-two years later, his hands shake from Parkinson's as he lights the Olympic flame.
9. He never found the kid who stole his bike.

## 7. Self-critique (honest)
- Opening: specific and funny; "whup" is his own word. Risk: 1954 + bike may feel slow for a Short;
  the kid's fury on screen must carry the first second.
- Line 3 is pure information (Olympics + title in one breath). Kept because it's fast and line 4 turns
  immediately; visuals must make it punchy, not a résumé.
- Line 5 is the heart and the longest line; it earns its length with the irony. Watch the pacing in the read.
- Cut from earlier drafts: "the greatest" (cliché), the Liston odds (hard to state exactly),
  "I ain't got no quarrel with them Viet Cong" (wording varies across sources), the medal-in-the-river myth.
- Ending: deliberately quiet. Some viewers may expect a triumphant final line; the callback is the
  stronger, less generic choice and pays off the hook.
- Remaining weakness: Frazier and the Supreme Court share one line, so both moments are brief.
  If the read runs long, line 6 is the first to trim.
